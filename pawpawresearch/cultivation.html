Cultivation & Recommended Varieties

Growing pawpaw trees is rewarding but requires attention to a few key requirements. Seedlings may take 5–8 years to fruit; grafted trees of named cultivars often produce sooner. Cross-pollination between genetically different trees is necessary for good fruit set.

Site Selection & Planting

  • Choose a location with moist, fertile, well-drained soil and protection from strong winds.
  • Young trees prefer partial shade for the first few years; mature trees fruit best with more sunlight.
  • Plant at least two genetically different trees within 30–50 feet of each other for pollination.
  • Mulch well to conserve moisture and suppress weeds; avoid deep cultivation near roots.
  • USDA hardiness zones 5–8 are generally reliable; some success is reported in zone 4b with protection.

Pollination Tips

Natural pollinators are primarily small beetles. In low-insect environments, growers sometimes hand-pollinate or use mild attractants. Avoid insecticides during bloom. Research continues on the most effective ways to improve fruit set without constant hand labor.

Selected Cultivars

Many named cultivars have been selected for larger fruit, better flavor, higher yields, or regional adaptation. A few widely discussed examples include:

  • Sunflower — Large fruit, good flavor, relatively productive.
  • Overleese — Classic selection with excellent taste and medium-to-large fruit.
  • Shenandoah — Known for large fruit size and good productivity.
  • Susquehanna — Thick flesh, fewer seeds, and rich flavor.
  • NC-1 and various Kentucky State University releases — Valuable for breeding and regional trials.

Always source plants or seeds from reputable nurseries that maintain accurate cultivar identity. Wild-collected seed produces variable offspring and should be used primarily for conservation or rootstock purposes.

Harvest & Use

Fruit is ready when it softens slightly and develops a fragrant aroma. It bruises easily and has a short shelf life at room temperature, so prompt use or refrigeration is essential. The soft pulp can be eaten fresh, frozen, or processed into ice cream, baked goods, and beverages.
