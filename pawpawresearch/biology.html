Biology of the North American Pawpaw

Asimina triloba, commonly known as the American pawpaw, is a small deciduous tree or large shrub native to eastern North America. It belongs to the Annonaceae (custard-apple) family and is the only member of that largely tropical family that thrives in temperate climates.

Physical Description

Mature trees typically reach 15 to 30 feet (4.5–9 m) in height, occasionally up to 40 feet under ideal conditions. The large, oblong leaves can measure 10–12 inches long and turn a clear yellow in autumn. Flowers appear in early spring before or with the emerging leaves; they are maroon to purple, roughly 1–2 inches across, and have a slightly fetid odor that attracts certain beetles and flies.

Fruit and Seeds

The fruit is a large berry, often 3–6 inches long, with thin green-to-yellow skin that softens and may develop brown patches when fully ripe. The flesh is creamy yellow to orange, soft, and aromatic. Large, dark, bean-shaped seeds are embedded throughout the pulp. Flavor is commonly compared to a blend of banana, mango, and melon, with a texture similar to custard.

Habitat and Ecology

Pawpaws prefer moist, well-drained, fertile soils and often grow as understory trees along streams, river bottoms, and rich woodlands. They tolerate partial shade well when young but produce more fruit in higher light once established. Trees frequently form clonal colonies through root suckers, creating the classic “pawpaw patch.”

The species ranges from southern Ontario and New York southward to northern Florida and westward to eastern Nebraska and Texas. Although globally secure (NatureServe G5), it is listed as threatened or endangered in some northern states and is considered sensitive in parts of Canada.

Ecological Role

Pawpaw leaves are the primary food source for the zebra swallowtail butterfly (Eurytides marcellus). The fruit provides food for a variety of mammals and birds. Because of its clonal growth and limited seed dispersal in modern landscapes, conservation of remaining wild patches is important for maintaining genetic diversity.
